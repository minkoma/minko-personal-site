Six ways to present the Bits of me photos

Same nine bits, same 27 photos, same stories. Only the photo presentation changes. The critters, the hidden surprises and the counter are untouched by all six: every option below leaves a card or a row that an egg can still be attached to, and option 6 gives the critter more room than it has now.

What is wrong with the current board is not the shapes, it is the count. Nine cards show all 27 photos at once, at six different silhouettes (circle, heart, arch, stamp, blob, plate, hoop, cloud), each one cropping a portrait phone photo into something that is not its own shape. So the eye gets 27 competing outlines and no single photo large enough to read. Every option below fixes that by changing one of two things: how many photos are visible at rest, or whether a photo keeps its own rectangle.

All six use your real photos from js/content.js, and all six read each photo's true width and height at runtime and let the picture set its box. Nothing here is squeezed into a square unless the note says so (option 6's covers, on purpose). Hover and click are wired up — these are working layouts, not mockups.

1

Photo deck

closest to what you have

Each bit becomes one polaroid with the rest of its photos stacked behind it. You see nine pictures instead of 27, each one four times the size it is now, and the travel bit still announces that it holds five. Click the stack and the top photo goes to the back, so all 27 are still reachable without any of them fighting for attention. The white border, the rotation and the shadow are the parts of the current card you said you like, kept exactly. The trade is that a photo further down the deck is only seen by someone who clicks.

2

Bento tiles

the calm one

The current cards, with everything decorative removed: one rounded rectangle per bit at three sizes, title on the photo, story only on hover. This is the layout most 2025 portfolio sites use, and it is the quietest option on this page. It is also the only one that crops — a tall ballet photo in a wide tile loses its top and bottom, which is the thing you rejected in the timeline collage. Extra photos cross-fade while the pointer sits on a tile, so a tile with five photos does not pretend to have one. Worth looking at mainly to see how much calmer plain rectangles read; it costs the scrapbook character.

3

Sticker sheet

one sheet, no cards

Drop the nine cards entirely and spill every photo onto one sheet. Each photo is its own sticker at its own shape with a small white edge and a slight tilt; the first photo of each bit carries a handwritten label, and the rest are unlabelled, so the sheet reads as one collection rather than nine boxes. It is still busy — 27 photos is 27 photos — but the busy-ness now comes from the photos themselves rather than from nine different outlines, which is a different and much more pleasant kind of full. The cost: the stories have nowhere to live, so they would move into a hover or a tap, and the surprises would need to hang off the labels.

4

Contact strips

everything, calmly

One row per bit: the title and story on the left, then every photo of that bit at one shared height and its own natural width. Because the height is constant, a portrait photo is simply narrow and a landscape photo is simply wide, and nothing is cropped or masked. All 27 photos stay visible, yet the section is calm, because the eye follows nine straight lines instead of nine clusters. This is the best option if you want every photo to count and the stories to stay readable. It is also the tallest, so the section becomes a scroll.

5

The list

quietest, most grown-up

Nine lines of large type, no photos at all until you point at one — then that bit's photos appear under the cursor and follow it. From a standing start the section is just a tidy index, which is the complete opposite of the current board, and it makes the photos feel like a reward. Two honest problems: it needs a mouse, so on a phone it degrades to a plain list and the photos have to be shown some other way, and someone who never hovers never sees a single picture. Included because it is the clearest answer to “too busy”, not because I think it suits a fun-facts section.

6

Pinboard + panel

biggest photos

Split the glance from the look. Nine small pinned covers sit on a board; clicking one opens a wide panel below it with that bit's full photo set at proper size, the story, and a dedicated space for the critter — which currently has to share a card with three photos. The resting state is nine small squares, so the section is about a third of its current height, and the photos are the largest of any option here once a bit is open. The covers are the one place on this page where a photo is cropped to a square, and that is deliberate: a uniform grid of covers is what makes the board quiet. The surprise counter works unchanged, one egg per panel.

If you want to narrow it down

Pick by what you want the section to do. If it should still feel like a scrapbook page and you want one scroll, that is 1. If every photo must be visible and the stories must stay readable, that is 4. If you want the photos as large as possible and a shorter section, that is 6. 3 is the most fun and the least organised; 2 and 5 are the calm extremes and both give up the handmade feeling. Any of them can take a mixed approach — for instance 1's deck for the bits with many photos and 6's panel for the three single-photo bits.
