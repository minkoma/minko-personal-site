Six ways to show reels and films together

Six layouts for the same mixed set, built on self-hosted MP4 rather than any embed. The set here is deliberately not your current four films: six vertical reels, three landscape films and one 4:5 post, so each layout is judged at the volume you actually have. Four items makes every layout look fine; ten is where they separate.

The one decision underneath all six: when a tile's shape and a film's shape disagree, you either crop (fill the tile, lose the edges) or letterbox (keep the whole frame, accept black bars). A vertical reel cropped into a 16:9 tile loses roughly three quarters of the picture, which is what the current strip does. Every option below either gives each shape its own tile or letterboxes honestly, and the note on each one says which.

Two things to know while you look. The stills are stand-ins: they are photos from the site, not frames from the films, so judge the shape and the arrangement rather than the picture. Playback is wired up for real — every tile is a <video> that fetches nothing until it scrolls into view — so once an MP4 lands at assets/film/<name>.mp4 this same page previews it live, hover to play and click for sound. You will need one still per film grabbed at the shape it is shown in. That is a one-command job per film once the files are local, and it is what makes any of these look finished.

1

Two tracks

the safe answer

Reels and films get their own grid behind a switch, so no film is ever cropped or letterboxed: a 9:16 tile holds a 9:16 reel. The switch carries a count, which does the “I have made a lot of these” work without needing everything on screen at once. This is what most working video people do, and it is the easiest to maintain — a new reel is one more line of data and the layout does not shift. The cost is a click: whichever track is second is seen by fewer people, so the order matters.

2

Ratio wall

volume, at a glance

One wall, every tile at its own true shape — tall reels run tall, films run wide, nothing is cropped and nothing is letterboxed. It is the only option where the whole body of work is visible in one view, which is exactly the “look how much I made” effect you asked for, and the mix of shapes is what makes it look like a real body of work rather than a template. Built with the grid-row span trick rather than CSS masonry, which still only ships in one browser. The cost is order: a ragged bottom edge is part of the look, and a long title can push a column out of step.

3

Feature + rail

clearest hierarchy

Your strongest film plays large, letterboxed on a dark screen so it keeps its full frame, and the reels sit underneath as a swipeable rail of phone-shaped cards. A recruiter who watches one thing watches the right thing, and the rail still says there are plenty more. This is the standard showreel pattern, and it is the only option that makes an editorial choice for the viewer. The cost: the reels are small and read as supporting material, so it is the wrong pick if the vertical work is the work.

4

Phone shelf

most like your site

Reels sit in phone frames on a shelf and films in a little set with a stand, so the vertical shape stops being an awkward fit and becomes the point: this was made for a phone. It matches the scrapbook tone of the rest of the page better than any grid does, and for a marketing portfolio it quietly says you shoot for the platform the client is actually buying. The costs are real: the device frames spend pixels on bezels instead of picture, and mockup framing reads as dated to some people, so it is the most opinionated option here.

5

Snap feed

most immersive

A snap-scrolling column, one film at a time, with its note alongside — the TikTok reading habit, borrowed. Vertical work has never looked better than this, because each reel gets the full height of the panel. Two things to weigh. A landscape film in this column has to letterbox, so it comes out small. And it hides volume: you only learn there are ten films by scrolling through all ten, which is the opposite of what option 2 does. Good as a second step — grid to browse, feed to watch.

6

Contact sheet

most orderly

Every cell is the same width and its height follows the film's own ratio, printed on a sprocketed darkroom sheet with the frames desaturated until you hover. It keeps the film decoration you liked, and because the column width is fixed it stays tidy at any number of films — the grid that breaks least as the set grows. The bottoms line up by row, so tall and wide cells sit together without either being cut. Quieter than option 2 and less personal than option 4; the closest thing here to a professional index.

If you pick one, what I need from you

The files. One MP4 per film, H.264 + AAC, faststart, 1080×1920 for reels and 1920×1080 for films. Keep each one under about 20 MB: GitHub Pages refuses a single file over 100 MB and the repo is already around 115 MB, so a dozen films at 20 MB is the ceiling before this has to move to a real CDN (Bunny Stream from about $1/month, Cloudflare Stream at $5 per 1,000 minutes stored). Self-hosted MP4 starts faster than any embed and has no branding; what it gives up is adaptive bitrate, which does not matter for short films on a laptop.

Nothing else. I can pull a poster frame from each MP4 at the exact shape the layout shows, so the stills stop being repurposed photos. Drop the files in assets/film/ with the names in js/reel-lab.js and reload this page; every option becomes live and you can judge them with the real work in them.
