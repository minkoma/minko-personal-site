Five ways to show the films

Your four real films in every layout, each one playable, so you are judging the thing itself and not a sketch. The film decoration you liked is kept in options 1 to 4, in a different form each time: ticket stubs, a contact sheet, clapperboard slates, 35mm perforations. Option 5 swaps it for a different era on purpose, so you have something to react against.

One thing worth knowing before you pick. Your films are not one shape. Two are landscape, the Alumni Awards is a squarish Instagram post, and the Farmers' Market is a vertical reel. The current strip forces all four into the same 16:9 frame, which crops the vertical one badly. Each option below handles that differently, and that is the real decision.

Separately, and worth fixing whichever option wins: all four poster images are portrait photos (1439×2000, 900×1200, 1500×2000, 675×1200). Every 16:9 frame on the current site is therefore showing a thin horizontal band cut out of a tall photo, which is why some posters look oddly framed. The proper fix is a still grabbed from each film at the shape it will be displayed in. Ask me and I will pull those frames.

1

Cinema marquee

one big screen

One film plays large on a lit marquee screen; the other three wait below as torn ticket stubs. The decoration moves from the frame to the stubs: perforated tear edge, admit one, a numbered seat. Best when you want the newest or strongest film to dominate, and it gives a vertical reel the whole height of the screen instead of cropping it. The cost is that three films are reduced to text labels until clicked.

2

Contact sheet

all four at once

A darkroom contact sheet with sprocket holes running along the whole sheet. Every frame is the same size, the way a real sheet is printed, and the picture inside keeps its own shape: the two landscape films fill their frame, while the Instagram post and the vertical reel sit letterboxed against the negative instead of being cropped. It is the only option here where all four films are visible at once and none is cut. Posters stay desaturated until you hover, which is how a negative reads, and everything plays inline where it sits.

3

Reel index

matches the deck album

The same shape as your new deck reader: a numbered list on the left, a sticky player on the right. Clapperboard stripes stand in for the film strip. It will feel immediately familiar and it scales to twenty films without redesigning anything. The risk is repetition, since the very next section on the page already looks like this.

4

Editorial strips

most room to write

Full-width alternating rows, echoing your "path so far" notes, with real 35mm perforations along the top and bottom of each media panel. The films get the largest posters of any option and each one gets space for a proper sentence or two about what it was for, which is the one thing a marketing portfolio actually needs. It is also the longest, so the section becomes a scroll rather than a glance.

5

Tape shelf

different decoration

The film strip swapped for home video: a 4:3 CRT with scanlines, and a shelf of VHS cases with handwritten sticker labels underneath. Closer to a real scrapbook object than a film strip is, and it suits personal films more than client work. It is the most opinionated look on the page, which cuts both ways.
