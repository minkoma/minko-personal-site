Six overlapping piles, nothing to click

Round 1 answered the wrong question. You did not want fewer photos, you wanted the photos to stop fighting each other, and you want all of them visible at a glance. So every option on this page follows the same three rules, and they differ only in how the pile is arranged. Option 0 is the board you have now, rendered from the live stylesheet, so the comparison is against the real thing.

The set here is edited, as you asked. Out: Forky, the latte and the travel stamps. In: the harbour photo of you and your sister, and the one of you in costume with your teacher. The food bit keeps only the dish at the bottom of the old plate cluster, the 土豆丝 and 水煮牛肉. Six bits, 18 photos, and no bit carries more than four, which is what makes every option below tighter than it was a round ago. The two new files are converted and sized like the rest (twin4.jpg, ballet4.jpg, both 1200px on the long edge, originals kept and recorded in the manifest). js/content.js is untouched — every edit lives in js/bits-lab2.js, so the live board has not changed yet.

All 18 photos are visible at rest. No clicking, no hovering required, nothing hidden behind anything else. Every photo keeps its own shape. No circles, no hearts, no arches, no squares; the picture decides its box, measured from the file at runtime, the same way the timeline photos work. Mixed sizes and a tilt. Each pile has a big photo and small ones at angles, so nothing reads as level or boxy.

Hover a photo in any pile and it straightens, lifts, and comes to the front. That is the one established fix for an overlapping pile: it means a covered corner is one pointer-move away rather than a click away, so overlap costs you nothing. It is the behaviour the tilted-polaroid pile pattern has used for years. The other borrowed rule is the white border: two borderless photos on top of each other turn into one mush, so the polaroid margin is not decoration, it is the line that tells the eye where one picture ends. Overlap in all six options is a fixed sliver at the frame's edge, never a percentage of the picture, which is what stops a photo covering a face.

What you have

the baseline

The live board, rendered from the same markup and the same styles.css rules index.html uses, so you are comparing against the real thing rather than against a memory of it. The hearts, circles, arches, stamps, blobs, plates, the embroidery hoop and the cloud are all here. Two things are worth watching for as you scroll down from it: the number of different outlines competing in one glance, and how much of each portrait photo the mask has cut away. Note that the column spans in styles.css were tuned for nine bits, so with six the rows no longer fill exactly — that is this page, not a fault in the live board.

1

Path pile

your own mechanic, reused

This is the “My path so far” cluster applied to the bits, and generalised past three photos. Two loose columns, each new photo dropped so no two tops are ever level, every photo overlapping the one above it by a fixed sliver at the white edge. Because it is the layout already running on the timeline, the two sections would finally look like they belong to the same scrapbook, and the code is already written and already tested at mixed shapes. Trimming the set fixed its one weakness: at five photos the two columns ran long, and with nothing above four the tallest pile is now about twice the height of the shortest rather than four times.

2

Fan

shortest section

Every photo in a bit is drawn at one shared height and laid along a line, each overlapping its neighbour by about a third, like a hand of prints spread on a table. Because the height is shared, a portrait photo is simply narrow and a landscape one simply wide, and the row tilts as a whole rather than each photo tilting separately. Hovering the pile spreads the fan slightly apart, which is the standard behaviour for this pattern. This is the most compact of the six by a wide margin, and the most orderly — which may be a point against it, since you want casual.

3

Anchor

my pick

One photo large, the rest small and tucked onto its corners so they break its edge. The size difference does all the work: the eye lands on the big picture, then finds the others around it, so a five-photo bit no longer competes with itself. It is the only option where the sizes are genuinely mixed rather than merely varied, which is what makes a collage feel handmade instead of arranged. It also degrades gracefully: the two bits with one photo each (the food dish and the bear) simply get a big photo and look intentional rather than empty, which is a real problem in options 2 and 6. With no bit above four photos the hero now dominates every pile, which is exactly what this layout wants.

4

Tossed

messiest

Nothing aligned to anything: position, size and angle all come from a hash of the photo's index, so it looks like someone emptied an envelope of prints onto the table. The tilt range is wider here (up to 8°) and the sizes vary more. The arrangement is stable across reloads, so it is random-looking rather than actually random. This is the most fun and the least controlled — worth a look to see where “casual” starts tipping into “untidy”, which is roughly the complaint you have about the current board.

5

Zigzag

narrow columns

The photos fall down one narrow column, alternating left and right, each sitting on the shoulder of the one above with a generous overlap. The zigzag means a photo is never directly behind another, so the overlap is always at a corner. This is the option that survives a four-column board and a phone screen unchanged, because the pile is naturally narrow. The cost is height: at four photos a pile is still a long vertical ribbon, and the section is the tallest of the six.

6

Cascade

one streak per bit

Each photo steps one notch right and one notch down from the last, corner over corner, so a bit reads as a single diagonal streak and a stack of bits reads as a rhythm down the page. Wide and short, and the most obviously playful arrangement here. Two honest problems: the diagonal needs the full page width to work, so this one only reads at one bit per row, and a bit with a single photo has no diagonal to make, which leaves two of the six looking like a different design.

What I would do

Take 3 for the four bits with several photos and let the two single-photo bits be one large tilted print, which option 3 already does. If you would rather the section matched the timeline exactly, take 1 instead: it is literally the same layout code, so the two sections become one visual language and there is less to maintain. Either way the critters, the hidden surprises and the counter stay exactly as they are, because the card around the pile has not changed.
